Voice Recording Guide

TAOM (Tales From the Age of Men). Read once before the first session.

This guide covers what is true for every culture: what the recordings are for, what the game does with each kind of line, how to name the files, and what format to deliver. The per-culture scripts carry the actual lines. After this you only need the script itself.

What you are recording for

TAOM is a total conversion of Mount & Blade II: Bannerlord into Middle-earth. It replaces the game's world, factions, troops and equipment. Battles run to hundreds of soldiers a side, in real time, with the player standing in the line.

Your recordings become a combat voice set: the pool of sounds one culture's soldiers draw from during a battle. Every dwarf on the field, on both sides, pulls from the same set. Two things follow from that.

You are not playing a character. You are playing a hundred men at once. Vary the takes. Three readings of the same line should sound like three different soldiers, not one soldier recorded three times. Different pitch, different urgency, different amount of breath.

The game repeats you. If a line has one take, players hear that exact clip every single time. This is why the script asks for three to five takes of everything. The variation is the product.

How the game uses each kind of line

The game does not play lines from a script. It fires a named slot when something happens. Knowing what triggers a slot tells you what emotional state the line sits in, which is most of the direction you need.

Kind of lineWhen the game fires it
Effort and impact
grunt, jump, climb, focus		Involuntary, from the engine itself, when a soldier swings, jumps, climbs a siege ladder, or braces. Body sounds, not performance
PainThe moment a soldier takes a wound. Short, real, no acting
DeathThe moment he dies
StunA hit hard enough to stagger him
FearTAOM-specific: some enemies project dread, and a soldier near them may break. Fires rarely, so it lands as a moment. A flinch and a caught breath, not a scream
War cryUnprompted, and again when a rally goes up. This is the soldier choosing to make noise
VictoryAt the end of a battle his side won
DismayWhen his formation's morale breaks and men start running
Orders
advance, fall back, form up, and about thirty more		Shouted by a captain when the player or the AI gives that order. He is projecting across a field, over other men shouting
Multiplayer callsPlayer-to-player callouts in multiplayer only

The orders are shouted, and there are a lot of them. Roughly forty of the fifty lines in a culture script are a man yelling across open ground with a battle running. That is the dominant register and it should drive how you pitch the whole session.

The involuntary sounds are not performed. A grunt is the sound of effort, a pain cry is a reflex. The most common failure in game voice work is acting them. Do the thing, do not portray it.

Length limits, which the engine enforces

These are hard caps. A file over the limit gets cut off mid-word in play.

Kind of lineLimit
Shouted lines: war cries, orders, formation calls, death cries8 seconds
Everything else: grunts, effort, pain, stun, victory, dismay4 seconds

In practice nothing should come near these. A shouted order wants to be one to two seconds. The limit exists to catch mistakes, not as a target. The script marks the limit on every line so you never have to guess.

Delivery format

48 kHz, 24-bit, mono WAV. One file per take. Unprocessed.

Unprocessed means no compression, no limiting, no EQ, no de-essing, no reverb, no normalisation, no noise reduction. Send us the raw take. Everything the game needs doing to these files, we do at our end, and we cannot undo processing that is already baked in.

Do trim obvious dead air, and leave roughly a quarter second of silence before and after the line rather than cutting hard against the first consonant.

The engine itself is tolerant and would accept a good deal less than this. We ask for 48 kHz mono because the same recordings feed a second system later on, for spoken dialogue, which does want exactly that, and starting there means nothing gets resampled twice.

If your setup cannot do 24-bit, 16-bit at 48 kHz is fine. If it cannot do 48 kHz, tell us before the session rather than converting on your end.

File naming

This is the part that costs us real time if it goes wrong, because a mislabelled take is a take nobody can find a use for.

Every line in the script prints its filename stem. Add the take number and .wav:

<stem>_<take>.wav

dwarf_shieldwall_01.wav
dwarf_shieldwall_02.wav
dwarf_shieldwall_03.wav
dwarf_pain_01.wav

Take numbers are two digits, starting at 01. Do not rename, do not add your initials, do not add the date, do not put the line text in the filename. Just the stem and the number.

If you record an alternate reading you want us to hear, add _alt: dwarf_charge_02_alt.wav. If a take is a mistake, do not send it; do not send a file named dwarf_charge_02_bad.wav.

Session order and looking after your voice

The scripts are ordered deliberately. Work top to bottom.

  1. Wordless and effort first, while your voice is fresh and undamaged. Grunts, pain, stun, climbing.
  2. Orders and formations next. This is the bulk of the work and it is all projection.
  3. War cries, victory and death last. These are the most destructive lines in the script, and putting them at the end means the rasp they leave lands where it belongs. A war cry recorded on a tired throat sounds better than one recorded fresh. A grunt recorded on a tired throat does not.

Take the breaks. Two shorter sessions produce better material than one long one, and the death and war-cry blocks are worth their own day if your voice is not built for shouting.

What happens to the files afterwards

Short version, so "done" means something concrete:

  • We convert each take to the format the game loads and drop it into the module.
  • We register the takes as alternatives for their slot, so the game picks between them at random.
  • We run a field battle and listen for anything that lands wrong, then come back to you with a short pickup list if we need one.
